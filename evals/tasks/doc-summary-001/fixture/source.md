# Kestrel Bay Tidal Array Begins Supplying Power to Port Elsby

*The Elsby Coastal Review — synthetic article for SenseiBench. Every name, place and figure below is invented.*

The Kestrel Bay Tidal Array, the first tidal energy project on the Elsby coast, began supplying electricity to the local grid this week. The array sits two kilometres offshore from Port Elsby, a fishing town of roughly 21,000 people, and uses the strong currents that run through the narrow mouth of Kestrel Bay twice a day.

The project was built by Marrowfield Energy, a regional developer that until now had worked mainly on onshore wind. It is operated jointly with the Elsby Harbour Authority, which owns the seabed lease and will receive a share of the revenue. Construction took 26 months, slightly longer than the 22 months first planned, after storms in the second winter delayed the installation of the subsea cable.

The array has a total capacity of 18 megawatts. Marrowfield Energy says that at typical output it will supply enough electricity for about 9,000 homes, close to the number of households in Port Elsby and the two neighbouring villages combined. Unlike wind and solar power, tidal generation follows the predictable rhythm of the tides, so the operators can forecast its output years in advance. Grid managers have welcomed that, because it makes the supply easier to balance against demand.

The total cost of the project was 64 million pounds. Around a third of that came from a national clean-energy fund; the remainder was raised by Marrowfield Energy and a group of local investors. The harbour authority contributed the seabed lease rather than cash.

The project lead, engineer Dana Okafor, said the hardest part of the work had not been the turbines themselves but keeping the seabed foundations stable in fast-moving water. "The currents that make this site so good for power are the same currents that make it so hard to build in," she said. Her team spent the first year mapping the seabed before any foundation was placed.

Environmental monitoring will continue for at least five years. Sensors mounted on each foundation record the movement of fish and marine mammals around the turbines, and the data will be published annually by the Elsby Harbour Authority. Early results from the testing period showed no change in the numbers of seals using the bay, though the operators stress that it is too soon to draw firm conclusions.

Local reaction has been mostly positive. The town council has said that the lease income will help pay for repairs to the harbour wall, and several fishing crews have been hired to run maintenance boats out to the array. Some residents have raised concerns about the onshore substation built at the edge of the harbour, which they say is larger than the plans first suggested.

Marrowfield Energy has already applied for permission to extend the array. If approved, a second phase would add further turbines further out in the bay, with construction finished by 2029. The company says a decision from the planning authority is expected within the next year.
